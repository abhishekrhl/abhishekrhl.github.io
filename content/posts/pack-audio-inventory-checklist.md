### Why wav ↔ soft-cite alignment matters before dry Hugo

Ensuring every generated audio snippet maps strictly to its source text prevents hallucinated claims in the final build. The fundamental issue arises when an inference engine, such as one running locally via `/go/ollama`, generates speech based on a prompt that lacks sufficient grounding in the provided context. If the model is not constrained by a strict mapping between the audio waveform and the specific paragraph it represents, it may inadvertently introduce information that was not present in the source document. This is particularly dangerous in technical or informational contexts where accuracy is paramount. Misaligned waveforms risk propagating unverified statements when rendered via `/go/ollama` or similar local inference engines, effectively turning a reliable static site into a vehicle for potentially misleading audio content. The audio becomes a black box that speaks without reference, breaking the chain of trust established by the written text.

Alignment acts as a safety valve, ensuring the spoken output remains faithful to the documented script before any deployment. This concept is central to our workflow philosophy regarding synthetic media. We treat the soft-cite not merely as a hyperlink or a footnote in the code, but as a binding contract between the audio asset and its textual origin [CITE: desk practice — spoken claims inherit body soft-cites only]. If a claim in the script lacks a corresponding source reference marked by a filled soft-cite with a lookup hint, that segment of audio must be treated as suspect. By enforcing this alignment prior to the build process, we create a filter that stops unverified content from entering the pipeline. This proactive measure is far more effective than trying to audit the final site for inconsistencies after the fact, especially when dealing with dynamic or generated assets.

### Fail closed: missing soft-cite → omit spoken claim

Fail closed: missing soft-cite → omit spoken claim (no invention) is the governing principle for our audio processing strategy. Implement a strict rule where any spoken claim lacking a valid body soft-cite is omitted from the abstract — no invention [CITE: fail-closed — missing soft-cite omit spoken claim]. This approach prioritises safety and accuracy over completeness; it is better to have a site with slightly less audio coverage than one that contains unverified claims. Treat missing citations as a failure condition rather than a warning, ensuring no unverified claims reach production. In software engineering terms, this is a "fail-closed" system, where the default state in the face of uncertainty is to deny access or execution. This guarantees that the final site contains only content backed by explicit source references, reinforcing the credibility of the platform. If a model generates a sentence about a specific statistic or event without a corresponding citation in the source text, that audio clip must be discarded. We do not attempt to guess the source or patch the citation later; the absence of evidence is treated as evidence of absence regarding the validity of the claim.

### Dry Hugo still ≠ Pages publish

Dry Hugo still ≠ Pages publish is a critical distinction often overlooked in automated workflows. Running `hugo --minify` locally via `/go/hugo` is merely a validation gate and does not constitute a live deployment to `/go/cloudflare-pages` [CITE: desk practice — dry hugo --minify before human Pages push]. Many developers assume that if a local build completes without errors, the site is ready for public consumption. However, a successful dry run confirms structural integrity but leaves the actual hosting environment untouched until an explicit deploy command is issued. The local build validates that the inventory files are correct and that the soft-cite alignment checks have passed, but it does not push content to the edge network or configure the necessary caching strategies for performance. Do not assume local build success implies readiness for public consumption or edge caching strategies. There is a distinct phase between generating the static files locally and publishing them to a distributed edge network where they will be served to users globally. Each of these phases requires its own set of checks, and conflating them can lead to unexpected issues in production.
